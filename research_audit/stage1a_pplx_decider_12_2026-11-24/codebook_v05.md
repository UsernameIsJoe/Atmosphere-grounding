# Stage 1A pilot codebook v0.5 — Calm, Inviting, Safe

**Status:** Researcher-directed extraction codebook; DRAFT, not yet tested or approved for production.  
**Scope:** Place-attributed experience/atmosphere in original visitor reviews. The target segment supplies evidence; surrounding text may clarify referents but cannot silently supply additional target evidence.  
**Changes from v0.4:** The emotion definitions and boundaries below are unchanged. Directional evidence is now the sole research output; non-directional material is excluded, without requiring a distinction between `none` and `uncertain`. Additional E/T calibration examples are included. Prior development/audit cases are not used as examples.

## Evidence basis

An emotion adjective is **not required**. Count a reported experience, site-directed assessment, interpersonal interaction during the visit, or spatial/sensory/social condition when it has a **defensible relationship to the particular atmospheric dimension**. A mention of an object or unrelated life event alone is insufficient.

- **E — reported experience or site assessment:** The review directly says how the visitor experienced or appraised the place, an encountered interaction, or a condition that affected the visit. It need not use an explicit emotion word. A direct appraisal of the *place* can be E for inviting.
- **T — inference from a reported condition:** A place-related condition supports a plausible atmospheric relationship **not explicitly experienced or appraised** in the text. Record the specific connection and retain `T` provenance; never silently promote T to E.
- E and T indicate **how a claim is grounded**, not model confidence. A T-only claim is not an E observation. Keep the streams inspectable separately.

**Attribution:** Place/surroundings and visitor-facing people or services may be relevant; unrelated event stress, an exhibit's standalone quality, and a visitor's unrelated personal circumstances do not automatically characterize the place. Preserve spatial and temporal scope. Context may resolve references but not become unquoted target evidence.

## Emotion constructs and boundaries

### CALM

**Construct:** A visitor-experienced atmosphere of tranquility, low agitation, ease, quiet, or relaxation. **Opposition:** Reported disturbance, intrusive noise, chaotic activity, or place-induced stress/tension.

- Support: quiet/relaxing experience or a specifically reported sense of ease.
- Oppose: intrusive sound, disruption, agitation, or tension attributable to the site or its surroundings.
- **Boundary:** An empty garden, music, a busy event, or a nearby street is not inherently calm or stressful. A delay in someone's personal plans is not necessarily evidence about the atmosphere.

### INVITING

**Construct:** A visitor-experienced **appeal or desirability of approaching, entering, visiting, or staying at a place**, including welcome, inclusion, hospitality, comfort, and the perceived attractiveness of the place itself. **Opposition:** Site-directed deterrence or aversion, hostility, exclusion, discomfort, or a barrier that discourages visiting or staying.

- Support: direct positive appraisal of the place as attractive, appealing, engaging, or worth visiting; comfort in staying; welcoming interactions.
- Oppose: a site-directed recommendation against visiting **for a stated visitor purpose**, discouraging entry/use, interpersonal rejection, or an experienced barrier.
- **Boundary:** Place-level desirability can count even when hospitality is not described. **Fame alone** does not prove desirability; explicitly positive appraisal of the place may. An exhibit's quality, ticket price, or private activity outcome does not automatically characterize the place's inviting atmosphere unless the statement links it to visiting/staying. A staff interaction is evidence for that encounter, not proof of universal treatment.

### SAFE

**Construct:** A visitor-experienced sense of security and protection from harm **or credible interpersonal threat/intimidation**, plus directly reported exposure to a specific danger. **Opposition:** Reported hazards, threats, intimidation, or place-related fear that reasonably undermines a visitor's sense of security.

- Support: reported sense of safety, protective experience, or place-specific reassurance.
- Oppose: credible physical hazards, direct threats, or intimidating encounters such as aggressive confrontation directed toward visitors—even if no physical attack is reported.
- **Boundary:** Minor rudeness does not automatically imply unsafe; distinguish social unwelcomeness from intimidation or fear. Darkness, grime, nearby roads, security cameras and noise alone do not establish safety direction without a specific, relevant consequence or credible concern. Felt safety is not an independently verified crime-risk estimate.

## Directional evidence — the research output

Extract **defensible support or opposition for each emotion**, independently. A candidate is accepted only when all of these hold:

1. **Source:** The evidence is grounded in an exact, attributable span of the **target**. The whole review can resolve an ambiguous referent or cause but cannot contribute a separate, unquoted claim.
2. **Attribution:** The claim concerns this place, its accessible/surrounding conditions, its appeal for visitors, or an interaction encountered there; not merely the visitor's unrelated circumstances.
3. **Construct:** The claimed effect or assessment actually relates to **this emotion's definition**. Overall positive/negative review sentiment, object presence and stereotypes alone do not suffice.
4. **Direction:** The text clearly **supports** or **opposes** the construct, directly through an E experience/site appraisal or through a specific, traceable T inference. Explicit emotion adjectives are **not required**; indirect evidence is not automatically weaker evidence.

- **Supports:** A defensible place-attributed claim of the emotion's positive construct.
- **Opposes:** A defensible place-attributed claim of the emotion's opposition construct.
- **No directional evidence:** If neither can be defended, the segment contributes **no extracted claim for that emotion**. Do not spend the research task choosing between `none` and `uncertain`, and do not interpret exclusion as proof the place lacks that quality.

**Keep both sides when they coexist.** One segment can contain defensible support *and* opposition for the same emotion; preserve each cited claim separately with its own E/T tag rather than averaging, selecting a forced winner, or discarding the segment. Negation, comparisons, changing conditions and opposing experiences must retain their actual scope.

**E/T remains mandatory for each accepted claim.** Do not invent experiences for descriptive T evidence, and do not suppress well-grounded T evidence simply because it lacks a reported emotion.

## Balanced calibration examples

Authored illustrations below are **not human-adjudicated evaluation data**. They show the difference between a defensible direction and a non-directional fact; transfer the underlying relationship rather than copying words.

| Emotion | Target statement | Direction | Track | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calm | “I could finally unwind in the courtyard.” | Supports | E | Directly experienced ease at the place |
| Calm | “The garden is shielded from the road by a sound barrier, with no traffic noise at the seating area.” | Supports | T | A specific noise-reducing condition plausibly supports tranquility |
| Calm | “The engines beside the plaza kept startling us.” | Opposes | E | Visitor-experienced disturbance |
| Calm | “Delivery trucks idle and rev immediately beside the outdoor seating every few minutes.” | Opposes | T | Recurrent, intrusive sound condition at the place |
| Calm | “The garden contains six benches.” | — | — | A feature with no particular calm relationship |
| Inviting | “The square is beautiful, and I would happily return.” | Supports | E | Positive place-level appraisal and desire to visit |
| Inviting | “A clear public entrance connects the plaza to a seating courtyard without steps or gates.” | Supports | T | Specific ease of approach and staying |
| Inviting | “We left because the attendant made us feel unwelcome.” | Opposes | E | Reported exclusion |
| Inviting | “The building advertises public access, but its only public entrance is locked during visiting hours.” | Opposes | T | Specific practical barrier discouraging entry |
| Inviting | “The museum exhibits five kinds of minerals.” | — | — | Exhibit description without place-level desirability or visitor experience |
| Safe | “I felt secure walking there after dark.” | Supports | E | Explicit experienced security |
| Safe | “A raised pedestrian crossing is fully separated from the highway by solid guardrails.” | Supports | T | Specific protection from nearby traffic |
| Safe | “The attendant followed us and shouted threats until we left.” | Opposes | E | Experienced credible intimidation |
| Safe | “The play area has no fence and opens directly onto fast-moving traffic.” | Opposes | T | Specific pathway to physical harm |

### Important boundaries for applying the examples

**Do not equate E with emotional adjectives.** Direct place-level appraisal or a reported environmental/social effect is E even when no emotion word appears. **Do not equate T with generic speculation.** T requires a particular environmental, spatial or social relationship that warrants the specified direction.

**No directional evidence is not a research category.** It is simply the absence of an accepted directional claim. Preserve original segments and rejected candidates for later cascade/recall audits; do not expend interpretation effort distinguishing abstention types. A future cascade may recover evidence excluded by an initial pass.
